On optimistic versus pessimistic, since that's the real trade-off there.

Pessimistic means a spinner, then updating on the response. It never lies on screen, the code is simple, there's no rollback. But every action feels slow. On a network with three hundred milliseconds of latency, the app feels sluggish.

Optimistic means the interface changes immediately, with rollback on failure. It feels instant, and it's directly an improvement to interaction latency. But it's more complex, and above all it risks lying.

The criterion is the probability of success and the cost of a lie. And the interesting part is that my answer is differentiated per action, and even per context.

Cancelling: optimistic, it almost always succeeds. Joining a waitlist: optimistic, there's no scarcity. Booking a half-empty class: optimistic, a conflict is unlikely. Booking the last spot: cautious. Paying: pessimistic, never show a false confirmation of payment.

The refinement I'd add is modulating the optimism based on spots remaining. On the last few spots, an honest confirming in progress is better than a cheerful you're in, followed by actually, no.

[medium pause] And the point I don't want to miss: optimism doesn't remove failure handling, it makes failure more visible, because the user already saw success. So the rollback has to explain itself. That spot was just taken, here are three alternatives. Never a silent revert.

Then there's invalidation, which I'd argue is the real subject when people say caching.

After a successful mutation, how do you resynchronize? You can invalidate and refetch, which gets you the server's truth and means you can't get your local transformation wrong, but it's an extra round-trip. Or you can write the response into the cache, which is instant and costs zero requests, but you have to transform it correctly, and the rest of the screen can stay stale.

The criterion is whether the mutation affects data beyond what it returns. Here it does. A booking changes the session, but also my credits, my upcoming bookings, and possibly a waitlist. The response doesn't contain all of that.

So the answer is both. Optimistic write immediately for perception, the response written on success, then background invalidation of the impacted keys. The user sees it instantly, and global consistency restores itself silently.

The trap is keeping the pre-mutation snapshot, otherwise the rollback can't be clean. And if two optimistic mutations are in flight on the same data, a naive rollback of the first one wipes out the second. Which is why I'd use the mechanisms the library provides rather than hand-rolling it with local state.

[long pause] Let me talk about idempotency properly, because it's the piece I care most about.

The problem is this. The client sends the request. The server creates the booking. The response is lost, the network drops, the request times out. The client believes it failed and retries. Now there are two bookings and two charges.

Where is the key born? There are three options and two of them are wrong.

Generated by the server: impossible. The server cannot know that two distinct requests represent the same user intention.

Generated by the client at request time: broken, and this is the classic mistake. Every retry generates a new key, so every retry creates a new booking. You feel like you've solved the problem and you haven't touched it.

Generated by the client at the moment of intention, meaning the click: correct. The same key is reused for every attempt belonging to that intention.
