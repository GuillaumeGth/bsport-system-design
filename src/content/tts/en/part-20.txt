The booking funnel completion rate is the headline metric. Failure rate by error code: a rise in payment failures is an incident, even if no server went down. Perceived confirmation time, from click to feedback, not API latency. And the optimistic rollback rate, which I like a lot: if spots are being taken between render and click far more often than before, it means your real-time availability is lagging. That's a metric that diagnoses a technical layer through a user-visible symptom.

[medium pause] The point I'd close observability on is this. A frontend can be perfectly healthy, zero JavaScript errors, normal latency, every service green, and completely broken, because a button ended up invisible at one breakpoint or a condition blocked one step of the flow. No technical monitoring catches that. Only funnel completion does. So I'd alert on booking completion rate, not just error rate.

[long pause] On proof of concept versus production, since that's a trade-off you asked about.

What I'd defer for a proof of concept: full offline support, the real-time channel, server rendering and search indexing, virtualization, exhaustive internationalization, a complete design system.

What I'd never defer: double-booking protection through idempotency, clear typed error states, displaying correct times, never showing a false confirmation, basic security, and keyboard accessibility on the critical journeys.

[medium pause] The criterion behind that line is this. What's deferrable is what I can add later without breaking anything or misleading anyone. What isn't deferrable is anything whose absence produces a lie to the user, or loses the studio money.

And to connect the technical defects to business consequences: a slow or confusing funnel means abandonment, which means attendance drops. A double booking means a dispute, a refund, and lost trust. A phantom spot means a member standing in the room with no machine. A wrong timezone means a missed class. Mishandling the ineligible state means a missed sale. And no offline support means a member stuck at the front desk with no signal.

[medium pause] The posture I'd state on this: a proof of concept narrows scope, it doesn't lower correctness. Reducing what we support is legitimate. Reducing whether it's right isn't.

[long pause] If you push me on the backend side, here's roughly what I'd say, and where I'd draw the line.

On double booking, the guarantee can't come from the client. The server serializes concurrent writes, with an atomic operation along the lines of decrement if there's capacity left. What I take from that as a frontend engineer is that the server is the source of truth, and my job is to handle its refusal gracefully.

On hold and confirm, if payment takes time, the server places a temporary lock with an expiry, then confirms. Which is where the pending state and the countdown in my interface come from.

On consistency between payment and booking, across two services you'd use a saga, meaning compensable steps: if payment fails after the booking, a compensating action cancels or refunds. And the frontend consequence is direct: confirmation can be asynchronous, so I need a confirming state and a way to learn the outcome, whether that's the real-time channel, polling, or a notification.

On server-side caching, my availability numbers may be served from a cache. So a slightly stale value is normal, which loops right back to availability being a hint rather than a guarantee.
