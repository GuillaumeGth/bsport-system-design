There's a subtle case I'd raise myself: what do we do offline? You can't ask the server. Two honest options. Show the last known eligibility, explicitly marked as unconfirmed. Or embed an approximate copy of the rules and accept that it can be wrong, in which case the interface has to stay cautious rather than assertive. My preference: offline I'd show the last known eligibility, explicitly marked as unconfirmed, rather than re-deriving the rules client-side and lying confidently.

[short pause] Third, typed errors, because errors drive the interface. A four hundred with a message, or a machine-readable error code? The criterion is whether each cause calls for a different interface. Here it massively does.

Session full means roll back and offer the waitlist. That's retention. Seat taken means go back to the seat selector. That's experience. Already booked isn't an error at all, and I'll come back to that. No credits left means offer to buy a pack. That's revenue. Not covered by plan means offer an upgrade. That's revenue too. Booking window closed means tell them it opens on the third of August at nine, and offer a reminder. Cancellation too late means explain the policy, which saves a support ticket. And payment failed means offer another payment method. Revenue again.

Two points on that. The displayed message belongs to the client. The code is for the machine. The text has to be translated, adapted to the brand's tone, and paired with the right action. Rendering the server's raw message is a shortcut that produces incoherent interfaces and sometimes leaks information.

And already booked is not an error. It's the normal result of a successful retry or a double tap. Treating it as an error shows an alarming alert in a situation where everything is fine.

[medium pause] Put together: three of these codes are revenue opportunities rather than failures. And already booked isn't an error at all, it's usually a successful retry. Showing an error there scares the user for nothing.

[short pause] Fourth, time in the contract. Instants travel in UTC, the studio's timezone travels with the data, and rendering happens in that timezone. The device's own timezone is only used for relative displays, like in two hours, or to warn a member who's travelling. I'll expand on this later, but it's a field in the contract, so I'm stating it now.

[long pause] Next, where state lives. I'd separate it into four kinds, because I think that separation is what keeps a client application maintainable.

Server state: sessions, availability, seats, my bookings, my pack, the floor plan. I don't own it, it can be stale, it gets revalidated, it gets garbage collected.

Local UI state: whether a modal is open, which seat is hovered, the step in the flow, scroll position. Ephemeral, not shared, dies with the component.

Global client state: the auth session, the tenant theme, the locale, network status, the offline action queue. Cross-cutting, persistent, few consumers but scattered ones.

And URL state: the date, the filters, the selected class. Shareable, bookmarkable, survives a reload.

The criterion that sorts everything into one of those four is scope and lifecycle. Who needs it, and when does it die.

[medium pause] The classic mistake is putting server data in Redux. It has a completely different lifecycle, caching, staleness, background revalidation, garbage collection, and you end up reimplementing all of that by hand inside reducers. I keep the global store for cross-cutting client state and the offline queue.
